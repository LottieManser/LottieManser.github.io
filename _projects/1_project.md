Here is my thesis abstract:

Biological systems are complex. To achieve specific outcomes, such as forming a limb or firing a neuron, interconnected regulators interact in intricate, interdependent ways. The timing and speed of these events are crucial; some form of coordination is needed to orchestrate this dynamic behaviour. The question of how the speed (or tempo) of gene expression is controlled is particularly relevant in two areas of biology. In developmental biology, many gene regulatory programs are conserved across species, yet the timescales on which they operate can vary significantly. Understanding how tempo is modulated can thus offer insight into the evolution of development. In synthetic biology, by contrast, the goal is to build genetic programs from the ground up. Although synthetic clocks can now be constructed in organisms like bacteria, the ability to tune their tempo remains limited.

While experimental work has identified molecular mechanisms that control the timing of gene expression, the capacity of mathematical models to incorporate tempo variation without changing the underlying dynamics is less well understood. This thesis addresses that gap by developing a mathematical framework that links the function of gene regulatory networks to the shape of their gene expression orbits. This makes it possible to identify tempo changes as specific perturbations in a dynamical system that preserve its orbit structure.

Using this framework, I explore how tempo can be tuned in a range of gene regulatory networks, from synthetic systems like the repressilator to developmental programs governing motor neuron differentiation. I also examine how noise affects both function and tempo. Finally, I investigate how tempo might be modulated during brain development through epigenetic changes, offering a theoretical basis for how temporal control could contribute to the increased complexity of the human brain.
